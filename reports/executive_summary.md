# Executive summary: ad campaign and new-player retention

**To:** Growth PM · **From:** Product Analyst, Growth · **Date:** 2026-11-03

Full analysis: [report.html](report.html) (opens in any browser, works offline). Interactive version: `streamlit run dashboard/app.py`.

## Bottom line

**Keep the ad campaign running at today's budget, keep a small group of users as an always-on comparison, and test more ads per person before doubling the budget.** The campaign clearly brought in extra conversions, but this test never tried a higher budget, so it can't tell us whether more spend would pay off. **Keep the game's first gate at level 30**: the test of level 40 showed no sign of a retention benefit, and a setup problem means we can't yet say whether it does any harm. **Focus retention work on bringing new players back the day after they install**, which is where most of them are lost.

## What we found

1. **The campaign worked.** 2.55% of people who saw the ads converted, vs 1.79% of a similar group shown a public-service ad instead: 0.77 more conversions per 100 people (+0.77 pp, or +43%). We're 95% confident the true increase is between 0.59 and 0.94 per 100 people. That is roughly 4,300 extra conversions (likely 3,300 to 5,300) from 14.0 million ad impressions. Only about 30% of the ad group's conversions were extra; the rest would have happened anyway. Crediting the campaign with every conversion from people who saw an ad (last-touch) would overstate it about 3.3 times (likely 2.7 to 4.3 times).
2. **Whether doubling the budget pays off is still unknown.** If a conversion is worth USD 50 (a placeholder; there is no revenue data), the campaign pays for itself as long as 1,000 ad impressions cost less than about USD 15.49 (likely USD 11.83 to USD 18.85). The 66% of users who saw 20 ads or fewer show no lift we could measure (the 95% range runs from −0.14 to +0.09 pp). Almost all the extra conversions (98%, counted bucket by bucket) came from the 17% of ad viewers who saw 36 to 200 ads. But the people who see the most ads are also the most active and convert more anyway, so this shows where the effect sits, not what extra spend would buy. Only a proper test can answer that. Moving spend to particular days or hours isn't supported either: the one weekday pattern we found reflects users' habits, not a time slot we can buy.
3. **New players of the game leave early, and the least engaged leave first.** These numbers come from our mobile game (Cookie Cats), the company's other product, so the sizes will not carry over one-for-one to the app. 55.5% of new players don't come back the day after installing, and only 18.6% play on the seventh day. Almost half (47%) play fewer than 15 rounds in their first two weeks, and only 2.3% of them come back on day 7. Players who return on Day 1 are 4.6 times as likely to come back on day 7 (32.8% vs 7.2%). This shows who leaves, not why.
4. **Moving the first gate to level 40 shows no sign of a retention benefit; whether it does harm is unclear.** The straightforward comparison shows a lower day-7 return rate with the gate at level 40: 18.2% vs 19.0%. But the two groups weren't split evenly: the level-40 group got about 790 extra installs, and the surplus sits among people who played fewer than 15 rounds; even players who played too few rounds to get past the first gate came back slightly less often in that group. About 60% of the gap sits where the gate cannot act. The part the gate itself could explain is about 0.3 percentage points per install (about 32 fewer day-7 players per 10,000 installs), and we're 95% confident it lies between a 0.71-point drop and a 0.07-point gain, so it may be zero; even that part may not be the gate. Players who got far enough to meet the gate did play a little more with it at level 40 (median 75 vs 72 rounds).

## Risks

- **Money figures rest on a placeholder** (USD 50 per conversion); break-even moves in proportion to the real value.
- **The budget question is open**: the campaign's effect sits with very active users, so extra spend may buy less than the average suggests.
- **No dates in the data**, so we can't see whether the ad effect wears off.
- **Retention patterns are associations**, and part of the link between rounds played and coming back is built in (both are counted over the same two weeks): they show where to test, not guaranteed wins. They come from the game, so the sizes will differ for the app.
- **The gate test's uneven groups** leave its effect unconfirmed; keeping level 30 is the safe default regardless.
